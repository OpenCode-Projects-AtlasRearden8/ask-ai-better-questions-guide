# 4. Ask for a critique instead of an answer

## The trick that sounds wrong but isn't

Suppose you've already written something — a resume bullet, a cover letter, a listing title for the vintage lamp you're selling, an email to your kid's teacher. There are two ways to consult the model about it.

**Way one (what most people ask):**

> "Make my email better."

**Way two (rarer, dramatically better):**

> "Here's my email. Find the three biggest problems with it, ranked by how much they'd bother a busy teacher reading it on their phone. Don't rewrite it yet."

Way two will nearly always produce a more useful conversation, and it costs nothing. This chapter explains why the strangely-worded question wins, and how to weaponize it everywhere.

## "Critique" turns the model from waiter into co-worker

When you ask for an improvement or an answer, the model's job is to *add material*. Adds are safe, they're agreeable, and they're statistically inclined to be generic — the pile of "consider adding a call to action!" advice you'd find by googling for twenty minutes.

When you ask for a critique, the model's job changes: it has to judge. Judging forces specificity ("your second paragraph buries the one date the teacher needs") because vague judgments ("it's good but could be stronger") produce visibly useless critiques — and the model knows what a useful critique looks like, because the internet is full of them: peer reviews, editing notes, code reviews, coaching feedback.

So a critique request is secretly a **specificity machine**. You typed "find problems" and out came tailored, concrete, actionable feedback that *you* then decide what to do with. You stay the author; the model becomes your sharp-eyed editor.

## The pattern, generalized

The deep version of this isn't about emails. It's a posture switch you can use on almost anything:

| Ask for a *thing* | Ask for a *judgment of a thing* |
|---|---|
| "Write my resume summary" | "Here's my summary: what would make a hiring manager in nursing leadership distrust it?" |
| "Give me a price for my textbook" | "Here's the item, condition, and comparables I found — what's weakest about my pricing logic?" |
| "Explain my options for refilling my medication early" | "Here's my plan for asking my clinic — what am I misunderstanding, or what will they likely push back on?" |
| "Is this chess opening good for me?" | "Here's my game so far — find the moment it went wrong, and say why." |

The left column produces output you must trust or distrust on no evidence. The right column produces output you can **check** — against your own knowledge, against the counterexamples it names, against reality. Which matters doubly, and leads directly to the skepticism chapter at the end.

## Ready-to-steal critique questions

- "Find the three biggest weaknesses in this, ranked, and explain why each one matters."
- "What would a skeptical [hiring manager / potential buyer / landlord] doubt about this?"
- "Act as my editor: mark every sentence that's vague, bloated, or repetitive. Don't rewrite. Just mark."
- "Play Devil's advocate against my plan. What are the strongest arguments it fails?"
- "Read this like a person who's skim-reading on a phone at 7 a.m. Where do they drop off?"
- "Grade this letter from the perspective of the person receiving it, as a number 1–10, then justify with specific lines."
- "What's the most likely *misunderstanding* someone could take away from this text?"

The ingredient hiding under all of them: a **named reader or a named standard.** "Critique this" floats. "Critique this the way a skeptical nurse manager skimming 40 applicants would" bites.

## A discipline: critique *before* revise

Here's the workflow that quietly beats ninety percent of AI-assisted writing sessions:

1. **Write your own draft.** Rough is fine. Rough and yours is better than polished and generic.
2. **Ask for critique only.** Specifically forbid the rewrite: "Don't rewrite it, just diagnose." This single clause dramatically improves answer quality — it stops the model from sweeping your voice away in one polished swoop.
3. **Argue with it a little.** Agree with the critiques that sting and push back on the ones that don't: "Hmm, disagree on #2, the client is on our side — but #3 is fair."
4. **Then revise — yourself, or with the model's edits on your terms.**

If you take one line away: **make it find your faults before it tries to fix them.** An answer that only improves things can't tell you what to *keep*, and your voice lives in the keep.

> **The one-line version of this chapter:** *Stop asking for answers about work you've done, and start asking what's wrong with it. "Find the three biggest problems with this" is worth more than any "make it better."*
