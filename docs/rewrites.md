# Before & after: twelve question rewrites

Theory is nice; watching a weak question get rebuilt is nicer. Below, twelve questions in the wild, each diagnosed and rewritten. The fixes use only the six habits from this guide. You will notice the rewrites are *longer* than the originals — that's genuine, real, and typical: context costs words, and context is what the model is missing.

---

## 1. Career search — vague goal

**Before:**

> Write me a resume.

**What's missing:** Who you are, what job, what the resume is *for*, what's already in it.

**After:**

> I'm an RN with 6 years of med-surg experience applying for my first charge nurse role. Here's my current resume: [paste]. Rewrite the summary and work bullets to target charge nurse — emphasize team leadership and precepting, and keep it to one page. Don't invent anything; if a point needs info I haven't given you, ask me instead.

*(Bonus habit: the last sentence feeds the model a way to ask for context instead of guessing — chapter 2.)*

## 2. eBay seller — no constraints

**Before:**

> How do I get more views on eBay?

**What's missing:** What you sell, where your listing stands, and what you've already done.

**After:**

> I sell vintage kitchenware on eBay (about 40 active listings, ~20 views and 1 watch a week each). I've already done the basics — good photos, filled item specifics. Give me the 3 specific changes most likely to raise views for *this kind* of inventory, in the order I should try them, and what I'd measure to know each one worked.

## 3. School help — the dreaded "this"

**Before:**

> Is this good? [attachment: essay]

**What's wrong (and this one's sneaky):** "Good" against what standard? Which reader? What's on the rubric?

**After:**

> Here's my essay for a grade-10 English class. My teacher says she grades on stated thesis, evidence from the book, and sentence variety. As a strict grader: which of those three is my weakest, and why? Mark specific sentences. Don't rewrite anything yet.

*(Chapter 4 in action: critique before revise, with a named standard.)*

## 4. Health question — no stakes signal

**Before:**

> Can I take ibuprofen with my blood pressure pill?

**What's wrong:** High-stakes category, zero context, invites a confident one-liner that could be wrong *for you*.

**After:**

> I take [your medication, dose] for blood pressure. I understand from the label that anti-inflammatory painkillers can interact with it. In plain language: what's the general interaction concern with ibuprofen for people like me, and what should I ask my pharmacist to confirm before I ever take it?

**And then verify with your pharmacist or doctor regardless.** Educational information, not medical advice — this guide's chapter 7 applies double, and the answer you *act* on comes from a professional who knows your chart.

## 5. Moving/organizing — wants magic, gets mush

**Before:**

> Organize my closet.

**What's missing:** The model can't see, touch, or size anything — and "organize" is five different projects.

**After:**

> I have a 4-foot closet: hanging clothes (maybe 30 pieces), a shoe shelf, and a top shelf buried in large bins I can't see into. I have maybe 2 hours this Saturday and $40. Give me a step-by-step plan for that afternoon, with a triage rule for what to be ruthless about (I tend to keep things "in case"), and the one $40 item that would help most.

## 6. The one-word question

**Before:**

> LinkedIn?

**Why it's weak:** It's not even a question — the model has to invent your goal, your industry, and your starting point.

**After (noting what even a one-worder can become):**

> I'm updating my LinkedIn as part of a nursing job search this month. I already have a photo and headline. In order: what are the first three things a recruiter-visibility-minded RN should change on a neglected profile, and what does "good enough" (not perfect) look like for each?

*(A single word can still become a great question — context is effort, not talent.)*

## 7. Writing emails — no reader named

**Before:**

> Rewrite this email to sound more professional. [email]

**After:**

> Here's an email to my son's teacher about missed homework during his hospital stay. [paste]. She's answered quickly before but I've never asked for something like this. Make it more professional *without* making it stiff or turning it into an essay — she reads on her phone — and keep it to 120 words max.

## 8. Home repair — misdirected confidence

**Before:**

> Why is my faucet dripping?

**After:**

> Kitchen faucet — single-handle, roughly 8 years old, drips from the spout once every few seconds even when fully off. I'm moderately handy (done basic sink P-trap work, never opened a faucet). Walk me through the most likely causes for *this type*, ordered cheapest-to-check first, and tell me what tools I'd need on the counter before I start.

## 9. Research — the uncheckable citation

**Before:**

> Give me some studies about shift work and health.

**After:**

> I'm giving a short talk to my unit on night shift health. List the 5 most-cited findings or studies on shift work and health, with the exact citation (authors, journal, year) for each, and flag the ones most likely to have alternate interpretations. After the list, tell me: which single caveat should I state out loud in my talk?

*(Chapter 7: inviting the caveat on purpose, not hoping the model consults its conscience.)*

## 10. Big project — everything at once

**Before:**

> Help me plan a book about AI for nurses.

**After:**

> I'm outlining a beginner-level book about using AI in nursing life (scheduling, paperwork, learning, everyday life — never medical advice). Before anything else: give me 4 possible *shapes* this book could take (e.g., by workflow, by scenario, by skill, by tool), one paragraph each, with who each shape best serves. Then stop and wait; I'll pick before we go further.

*(Chapter 6: outline before output, stop and wait.)*

## 11. Money — vague urgency

**Before:**

> Should I refinance my mortgage right now?

**After:**

> Here's my situation: 30-year fixed at 6.8%, current balance about $184,000, 24 years left, planning to stay in the house at least 5 more years. Rates I'm seeing for a similar 30-year are around 6.1% with about $3,200 in closing costs. Walk me through whether that trade makes sense *for my numbers* — show the monthly saving, the break-even point, and what fact would flip the decision entirely.

*(Note the honest check on real figures: it's your numbers — chapter 2's paste-in — with the flip question from chapter 5.)*

## 12. The blank canvas — nothing typed yet

**Before:** *(nothing — you just open a new chat and wait to be inspired)*

**After:**

> Act as my sounding board. I'm an RN considering moving part of my side income from flipping vintage kitchenware into a small Etsy shop. Before I ask anything else: ask me 3 questions whose answers would most change your eventual advice.

*(The chapter 5 opener, turned around to interview *you*. Best first move when you don't know what your question is.)*

---

## The pattern underneath all twelve

- **Names a specific you** — role, experience, inventory, medication, time budget, dollars available.
- **States a goal worth measuring** — "raise views," "one page," "break-even point."
- **Pastes or points at the real material** — the resume, the figures, the faucet, the essay.
- **Invites correction or ask-back** — "if you need info, ask," "flag the caveats," "ask me 3 questions."
- **Scoped small enough to answer well** — the *exact week*, the *first three things*, *the afternoon*, not life itself.

The [glossary](glossary.md) is there for any term that still feels fuzzy. But the fastest way to make these patterns *yours* is the drill yard: [practice drills](drills.md).
