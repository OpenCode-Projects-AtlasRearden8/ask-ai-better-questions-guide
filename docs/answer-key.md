# Answer key

No peeking before you've tried. Full credit is not about matching these answers word for word — it's about capturing the *habit*. Read each: what earns credit, and what to do on a miss.

---

## Block A — Adding context

**A1.** Full credit for two lead-in sentences of substance, e.g.: *"I called because a card charge appeared twice — same amount ($42.19), same day, only on my bank's page, and I have the receipt."* or *"I'd like a polite but firm email to the manager as a last stop before I file a chargeback."* The habit: **say who you are, what happened, and what you want**, before the actual request.

*On a miss:* You wrote a polite framing instead of facts. "Please be nice about this" is not context; names, numbers, and goals are. Re-read the [context starter sentences](02-give-the-model-context.md#context-sentence-starters-steal-these).

**A2.** The missing piece: **what to preserve.** "Improve this" invites a total rewrite, because the model has no definition of what "working" means. One sentence — "keep my opening story and my voice exactly; make the rest tighter" — fixes it.

*On a miss:* If you answered "more context," half credit — that's the chapter, but the *specific* missing context is the success test. What should stay? What does "better" mean?

**A3.** Credit for naming a level and its consequences, e.g.: *"I've never run one and my tech experience is basically installing apps. Explain like I'm brand new, define any term before using it, and no code unless you explain it."* Bonus credit for using the model's own response to calibrate: *"If a sentence would baffle a non-technical friend of mine, rewrite it."*

*On a miss:* "Explain it simply" is the common answer; it's worth half credit at best. "Simply" is relative — to what, a robot? Name the human you're writing for or against.

## Block B — Layered follow-ups

**B1.** Full credit for one message doing three jobs: correct, constrain, and re-aim. E.g.: *"The shrimp won't work — two family members won't touch seafood. Redo the week with chicken, pasta, and eggs as the backbone, keep it to the same format, and just tell me the swaps for Wed onward. Also, sauces alright but nothing spicy."*

*On a miss:* "No shrimp please" is a fair start but earns half credit: it corrects without re-aiming, so round two will miss something else. One message, three jobs.

**B2.** A **narrowing follow-up**. What it prevents: the next giant unfocused answer — it stops the model from re-dumping everything and puts its attention where yours actually is. Bonus credit for adding: "half as long, same depth on point 3."

*On a miss:* If you said "critique," over to chapter 4 — different job. Narrowing moves attention; critique changes the *job* from producing to judging.

**B3.** Credit for a first message that's Layer 1 (the map, not the price). E.g.: *"I need to pick between a used minivan and a used SUV — two kids, a 70-mile round-trip commute, no towing ever. Before comparing models: what are the 3–4 trade-off axes that should decide this for a family like mine? Don't recommend a car yet."*

*On a miss:* If yours compared gas mileage models directly, you skipped to Layer 2 — which is fine *later*, but the layered habit means you establish the decision axes first, so the model isn't comparing while you're still deciding what matters.

## Block C — Critique instead of answer

**C1.** Credit for critique-first phrasing with a named standard, e.g.: *"Here's my apartment-hunting checklist: [paste]. As a skeptical landlord-tenant lawyer would: what's missing, what's wishy-washy, and which item would I regret having skipped at move-out? Rank the top three. Don't add new items yet."*

*On a miss:* "What's missing from my checklist?" is decent — half credit. The full-credit move has (1) a named skeptical reader, (2) a ranked ask, and (3) the "don't rewrite yet" clause.

**C2.** The clause: ***"Don't rewrite it — diagnose it."*** (Also fine in any phrasing: "just mark the problems," "advise, don't retype.") The model instead becomes an editor that points at sentences and explains what's weak, and *you* stay author.

*On a miss:* "Be more specific" is hopeful but unfocused — the swap from answer→critique is what actually changes behavior.

**C3.** The named reader is **your grandmother, as herself** — sharp, clutter-averse, emoji-hostile. E.g.: *"Critique this birthday message as an 80-year-old who's sharper than she lets on, hates clutter, and can smell gibberish. Mark anything that's sappy or padded."* Full credit for *any* concrete, real-person reader. The lesson generalizes: "reader" is wherever the standard lives — a busy teacher, a skeptical buyer, a tired charge nurse at 0300.

*On a miss:* "A stranger" or "a friend" are too vague to steer a rewrite. The reader is the steering wheel.

## Block D — What am I missing?

**D1.** Credit for a message that (a) states your plan including the assumption, and (b) explicitly asks the model to attack that assumption. E.g.: *"I'm about to sign a one-year warehouse lease at $1,100/mo for my eBay overflow. I've capped myself at $1,200/mo and assumed that's safe because my sales have grown 15% each quarter. What am I missing? Challenge specifically whether my rent cap is realistic for someone whose inventory is seasonal — holiday Q4, dead February."* Bonus credit for targeting *your* assumption by name.

*On a miss:* A bare "what am I missing?" with no plan attached produces generic advice. The prompt only works when the model has your version of reality to poke.

**D2.** "What am I missing?" asks the model to **generate** your gaps from its side of the desk. "Ask me three questions" makes the model **interview you** — which surfaces what *it* is missing about your situation. The difference matters when your situation has private, specific facts the model couldn't infer (your lease's storage clause, your real Q4 sales history, your mom's actual kitchen).

*On a miss:* If you answered "they're basically the same," re-read the [devious-and-excellent prompt](05-what-am-i-missing.md#the-missing-family-of-prompts) — the interview move is how an abstract topic becomes *your* topic.

**D3.** The version: *"Here's my understanding of night shifts and my sleep: I think napping before shift makes it basically fine, because my body gets its full sleep split across two blocks. Where is that picture wrong or incomplete?"* Credit for the three-part structure — claim, stated reasoning, open challenge — because it's the "checking your version against the model's knowledge" move from chapter 5's last section.

*On a miss:* "I think I'm fine — tell me if I'm wrong" is the casual version, worth half credit. The stated reasoning is what gives the model something specific to fix.

## Block E — Steering long tasks

**E1.** The five nouns, in moving-day words:
1. **Goal:** "a check-off-able plan to pack, label, and load (unload is Sunday) Mom's kitchen in one weekend — bins, boxes, tape, labels."
2. **Audience:** "two adults who don't know her kitchen, plus Mom, who gets overwhelmed by clutter."
3. **Constraints:** "one weekend, one 10×10 storage unit, $150 in supplies, sore back permitted."
4. **Ingredients:** "here's the rough inventory: roughly [counts and quirks of pans, china, gadgets]."
5. **Check-ins:** "outline the plan first and wait for my OK."

*On a miss:* "A packing plan" is the goal — alone, it earns one-fifth credit. The other four nouns are where long-task briefing pays.

**E2.** The intercept, any phrasing with all three jobs — **stop, keep, redirect:** *"Stop there. Pages 1–2 are right; page 3 drifted into general moving advice. Re-read my original brief, then finish page 3 on the kitchen, close on the truck-loading order, and give me a one-line note about what changed on page 3."* Bonus credit for the audit note — it keeps *you* honest about the drift too.

*On a miss:* A bare "stop" wastes your intercept; the next reply will summarize rather than resume. And a bare "keep going" isn't steering at all.

**E3.** The reset, full credit shape: *"Let's restart cleanly. Situation: [brief, improved brief — you've learned a lot]. What's worth keeping from the last chat: [paste the good parts]. Now produce [the goal] from that foundation, in one pass."*

*On a miss:* "Just rewrite it all" doesn't carry over the good parts — or your corrections — and you'll re-tangle by Tuesday. The reset needs three things: a fresh brief, the salvage list, and the ask.

## Block F — Skepticism

**F1.**
- (f) Empire State Building, 1,454 ft with antenna: **low**. Concrete, famous, non-load-bearing (no action rides on it), and easy to verify if you ever care. Skepticism is for when it matters.
- (g) "your eBay sales tax rate will be exactly 7.25%": **high**. It's *your-jurisdiction* specifics — money-binding, varies by what and where you ship, and the exact confidence is exactly the costume hallucination wears. Verify with eBay's tax settings and a state source.
- (h) Box-labeling checklist: **low**. Generic household advice; wrong details are trivially visible and cheap to fix.

**F2.** Search the title — or the journal, year, and author — in an actual search engine. Ten seconds. If the paper doesn't surface, or surfaces with different authors, you just prevented the single most public form of AI embarrassment. (If it *does* exist, skim enough to confirm it says what you're about to claim it says.)

*On a miss:* Asking the model "are you sure it's real?" — worth a little, but the model is the thing under suspicion; a model can't verify itself.

**F3.** **False.** Repetition is consistency, not verification. The model isn't consulting a database — it's generating the most plausible next words, and the same training can produce the same wrong answer three times running. Consistency *raises* your baseline trust and is worth something, but for anything you'd sign, pay for, or put your name on, it's an outside source that counts: the [triage table](07-when-to-be-skeptical.md#the-danger-zones-a-triage-table) tells you which sources.

*On a miss:* This is the most common false-belief in this guide — recognized. Now that you know it, you're ahead of most of the internet.

## Scoring

- **10–12 full credits:** you've got the habits; start using them as a default and notice the shift in your answers.
- **6–9 full credits:** solid. Re-read the chapters behind your misses — the pattern is usually *missing reader* or *missing constraint*.
- **0–5 full credits:** genuinely normal for a first pass, and notice the misses cluster: that's the six habits telling you which chapters are *yours* to work on. The guide is short — reread those two.
